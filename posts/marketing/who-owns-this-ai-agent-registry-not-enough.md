# Who owns this agent? Why a registry isn't enough

**A registry records which agents existed at the moment somebody wrote it down.** Ownership
has to be enforced by the system that actually runs the agents, or it drifts the instant
reality changes — and reality changes without asking the spreadsheet.

The failure is not that teams are careless. It is that a registry is a *description* sitting
beside the system, and descriptions decay. An engineer spins up an agent on Friday to
unblock a release. It works. It stays. It is never added to the list, because adding it to
the list is a separate act of discipline with no immediate consequence for skipping it.
Multiply by a team, then by a quarter. IBM's Institute for Business Value reported at Think 2026
that **18% of organizations maintain a current and complete AI inventory**
([IBM, 11 May 2026](https://www.ibm.com/think/news/think-2026-ai-recap)).

The alternative is that ownership is a property of the system where agents are created, not a
note about it. If creating an agent means choosing the role it will hold inside an
organization, the inventory is not a separate chore — it is a by-product of creating the agent.
You are not reading a record of what someone believed last quarter. That is the model agent.ceo
uses: you add an agent by [choosing a role for your organization](/agents), and the
organization's registry lists the agents registered to it.

The practical test for any tool claiming to solve this: **can an agent run without being in
the inventory?** If yes, the inventory is documentation, and it will be wrong. Ask every vendor
that question, including us, and ask to see the answer rather than hear it.

**The honest boundary:** a role-first model costs you something a spreadsheet does not — you
have to declare the role before the agent runs. That friction is the point, and if your team
genuinely only spins up an agent once a month, the spreadsheet is fine.
